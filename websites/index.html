What your site has to do

Four jobs. A site that does the first two badly is decoration; a site that does the last two badly becomes someone else’s asset.

A page for each thing you sell

Someone searching for one specific service should land on a page about that service, not a homepage that mentions it third. It ranks better, it converts better, and you can tell which ones are working.

Written in the words people search

You call it reactive maintenance; they type “emergency plumber”. When the two differ, the page uses theirs. The trade language can stay for the people who already know you.

Accessible is not a bolt-on

Real headings, visible focus states, AA contrast, and a site that works from a keyboard. A site a screen reader can follow is a site Google can follow, so it is never a separate line on the invoice.

The technical part, done quietly

Sitemap, canonical tags, structured data, one main heading per page, descriptions written rather than generated. None of it is clever and all of it is missing from most small business sites.

One obvious next step per page

Ring, book, or send a photo of the problem — pick one per page and make it the loudest thing on it. Pages offering four equal options get fewer of all four.

The answer before the objection

Roughly what it costs, how long it takes, what is included, what happens if it goes wrong. People who cannot find those answers do not email to ask — they leave.

Contact that works on a phone

A number that dials when tapped, an address that opens a map, and a form short enough to finish one-handed on a bus.

Proof you actually have

Photographs of your own work, reviews with real names attached, the trade bodies you are genuinely a member of. No stock photos of people in headsets, and no logos of companies you once quoted for.

Hand-built, not assembled

No theme to fight, no builder to outgrow, no plugin that stops being maintained in two years. The page that reaches the visitor is close to the page that was written, which is why it arrives before they have decided to leave.

Secure by having less

Static pages with no database and no admin login remove most of what gets attacked. Security headers, enforced HTTPS and a locked-down content policy come as standard, not as an upgrade.

Images that are not four megabytes

Resized to the size they are shown at, converted to a modern format, and loaded late if they sit below the fold.

Nothing loaded that you did not ask for

Every third-party script is a request to someone else’s server, a privacy question and a thing that can break. Analytics, chat widgets and embeds go on because you decided they should, not because a theme shipped with them.

Questions worth asking

The ones that decide whether a site is an asset or a subscription.

Why separate pages instead of one long homepage?

Because people search for one specific thing. A page that answers exactly that question is more likely to be found and far more likely to convert than a homepage that mentions eight services in passing. It is also easier to tell what is working, because each page has one job.

Do you use WordPress or a page builder?

Only when a site genuinely needs one — frequent publishing, a shop, several editors. Most small business sites are faster, safer and cheaper to run as hand-built pages with no plugins to update and no login to attack.

Will I be able to edit it myself?

Yes. If you want to make routine changes, I connect a simple editor to the pages that change often and leave the rest as code so the site stays fast. You will not need to learn anything to change your opening hours.

How long does it take?

A single landing page is usually a week or two. A four-to-eight page site is typically four to six weeks, and the slow part is almost always the words rather than the build — agreeing what each page is for takes longer than making it. If you already know what you want to say, it moves faster.

What do you need from me?

An hour on a call, photographs of your own work if you have them, and someone who can answer questions about how the business actually operates. You do not need to write anything; I draft the copy and you correct it, which is far easier than starting from a blank page.

Is there a monthly fee?

Not to me. Hosting for a site like this is usually free or a few pounds a month, billed to your own account. Ongoing care — changes, monitoring, new pages — is optional, separate, and easy to stop.
